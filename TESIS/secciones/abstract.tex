\begin{center}
\large \vspace{-1.5cm} \textbf{Urban traffic optimization models in Lima with intelligent traffic signals through Reinforcement Learning (RL) and SUMO simulation}
\end{center}

Lima Metropolitana has critical congestion points at high-traffic intersections and corridors. This thesis asks whether a traffic signal controller that learns with \textbf{Reinforcement Learning (RL)} and receives information from its neighbouring signals reduces the \textbf{delay per vehicle} with respect to a well-tuned fixed-time plan, without falling behind actuated control, the two usual references in traffic engineering.

A test bench was built in \textbf{SUMO} with three synthetic networks (a two-signal corridor, a three-signal network with a roundabout and a nine-signal grid), each under nominal and high demand: six cases. The agents, one per signal, and the message exchange between neighbours (IPPO with messages) were programmed from scratch and compared with IPPO without messages, MAPPO, Double DQN, Q-learning and SARSA. The evaluation has three steps of increasing stringency. The first study uses vehicles that arrive at regular intervals, and a stricter measurement repeats it, without retraining, with total delay and a fixed-time plan tuned from the minimum green. The second study uses random arrivals, re-tunes the fixed-time plan to the second for each case, publishes its rules before training and is evaluated blind, with two independent sets of 100 seeds. Its protocol is also applied to two places in Miraflores, Lima, built from the real geometry and published volumes: a junction, the Óvalo Vasco, where a single controller runs four intersections, with its signal plan measured on site, and a six-intersection corridor.

With regular arrivals, the model reduces delay by 6.83\% against the tuned fixed-time plan of the first study and by 14.35\% against actuated control. Under the stricter measurement, the advantage over fixed time disappears ($-0.03$\%), and the second study confirms it: with regular arrivals, a well-tuned fixed-time plan is nearly optimal and the model ties with it ($-0.4$\% in both sets).

With random arrivals, IPPO with messages, trained with that traffic, outperforms the fixed-time plan tuned to the second in 3 of the 6 cases: the corridor under both demands and the grid under high demand. In those cases the full model, a variant that also observes nearby vehicles, reduces total delay by 16 to 27\% and also has less delay than tuned actuated control. These are the same cases in which tuned actuated control improves on fixed time, that is, those in which reacting to traffic reduces delay. In the roundabout network and in the grid under nominal demand, the model ties with fixed time or has up to 1.6\% more delay. Summarised in a single number, the change against the fixed-time plan tuned to the second is $-10.8$\% in the first set of seeds and $-11.1$\% in the second. The target of a 25--40\% delay reduction is reached only in the grid under high demand.

In the Lima corridor, the model reduces total delay against the fixed-time plan tuned to the second by 4\% under the published demand and by 8\% with 1.2 times that demand, and it also outperforms tuned actuated control; that advantage comes from the messages between intersections. In the junction it reduces delay by 9\% under the published demand, with the same delay as tuned actuated control, and has 3\% more delay under the high demand, where fixed time uses 77-second greens that the model cannot give. Against the current plan of the junction, measured on site, the model reduces delay by 23 to 24\% under the published demand and by 50\% under the high demand, and, if demand is split differently among the streets, by 30 and 43\% against the fixed-time plan tuned to the second. A variant that also chooses the order of the signal states, the one the protocol cited for the junction, did not help at that site. Demand in those cases is partly assumed and was not calibrated with traffic data.
