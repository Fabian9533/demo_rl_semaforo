\begin{center}
\large \vspace{-1.5cm} \textbf{Urban traffic optimization in Lima with intelligent traffic signals through Reinforcement Learning (RL) and SUMO simulation}
\end{center}

Lima Metropolitana has critical points of traffic congestion, especially at high-traffic intersections and urban corridors. This research proposes to \textbf{reduce the average delay per vehicle} through \textbf{adaptive traffic signal control} with \textbf{Reinforcement Learning (RL)} and validation in \textbf{SUMO}.

The solution is a reproducible pipeline with four units: data and preprocessing (U1), \textbf{RL} agents, one per intersection (U2), simulation and measurement in SUMO (U3) and \textbf{distributed coordination between traffic signals} (U4), in which each agent exchanges messages with its neighbours to build green waves and to prevent queues from blocking the neighbouring intersection (\textit{spillback}). It is evaluated on six synthetic cases of increasing difficulty: a two-intersection corridor under nominal (P1) and double demand (P2), a three-intersection network with a roundabout under nominal demand (P3) and 1.2 times the nominal demand (P4), and a nine-intersection grid under nominal (P5) and double demand (P6). The real intersection in Lima, imported from OpenStreetMap but not yet cleaned, remains as future work.

In every case, a fixed-time program tuned by grid search and SUMO's native actuated control are compared with tabular Q-learning and SARSA, Double DQN, MAPPO with a centralised critic, and IPPO (an independent PPO per signal) in three variants: without messages, with neighbour messages and with a spillback penalty. Except for a Q-learning ablation with a queue reward, all learners use the delay reward. Each one is trained for 200 episodes with three base seeds; each training publishes the policy with the best validation score, measured on two dedicated validation seeds, and each model is represented by its median base seed according to that validation score. All are evaluated on the same thirty seeds, disjoint from the validation seeds. The primary metric is the \textbf{delay per vehicle} (SUMO's \texttt{timeLoss}), and a predefined verdict rule classifies each model in each case according to its change against fixed-time and actuated control and its statistical significance, or leaves it pending if data are missing or its policy did not converge.

IPPO with neighbour messages is the only model with a firm (not pending) verdict in P1 to P5 that fails in none: against fixed time it cuts delay by 6.8, 23.3, 8.8 and 3.6\% in P1 to P4 and ties with it in the grid (P5), and against actuated control it cuts delay by 3.6 to 16.4\% in those five cases. In the grid under double demand (P6) no learner improves on the tuned fixed-time program. There, IPPO with neighbour messages stays 2.2\% above fixed time with no significant difference, but its verdict is pending because its policy did not converge. In that case the actuated control collapses ($+73.3$\% against fixed time) and every evaluated learner beats it. The single summary number is the geometric mean of the change over the six cases: for IPPO with neighbour messages it is $-6.83$\% against fixed time and $-14.35$\% against actuated control, which collapses in P6. Without P6, over P1 to P5, it was $-8.55$ and $-7.72$\%. Without messages, IPPO ties or fails except in the corridor under double demand (P2). MAPPO gets the same verdict as IPPO with neighbour messages wherever its own is firm (it did not converge in P3, P4 and P6), and Double DQN falls behind. Q-learning and SARSA fail in every nominal-demand case, and an ablation with a queue reward shows that the reward is not the cause.

A robustness analysis repeats the comparison with total delay, which adds the wait to enter the network, and with a fixed-time program whose grid search starts at the 10~s minimum green. Under that measurement, IPPO with neighbour messages keeps a clear advantage over fixed time only in P1 ($-6.7$\%). In P3 it improves only slightly ($-0.5$\%) with a policy that gives the same result as a trivial control with every green at its minimum, and in P2, P4, P5 and P6 it stays between $+1.1$ and $+2.6$\% above fixed time. Over the six cases, its summary change is $-0.03$\% against fixed time (95\% CI $-0.56$ to $+0.53$\%) and $-11.45$\% against actuated control.

The targets are to reduce delay by \textbf{25--40\%} and queues by at least \textbf{20\%} with respect to fixed time. The delay target is not reached in any case, and IPPO with neighbour messages meets the queue target only in P3 and P5. The work supports the claim that, in corridors and networks of two or three signals, a learned coordination with neighbour messages beats both classical references by a measurable margin under the primary metric, and that in a nine-intersection grid it matches a coordinated fixed-time program; not that it reaches the size of the targets. Under double demand in that grid the tie is provisional, and against fixed time the advantage survives the robustness analysis only in P1.
