\begin{center}
\large \vspace{-1.5cm} \textbf{Urban traffic optimization in Lima with intelligent traffic signals through Reinforcement Learning (RL) and SUMO simulation}
\end{center}

Lima Metropolitana suffers severe congestion at high-flow intersections and corridors, which increases travel times and emissions and reduces urban mobility. This thesis aims to \textbf{reduce the average delay per vehicle} through \textbf{adaptive traffic signal control} based on \textbf{Reinforcement Learning (RL)}, validated in the \textbf{SUMO} microsimulator.

The solution is a reproducible pipeline with four units: data and preprocessing (U1), \textbf{RL} agents, one per intersection (U2), simulation and measurement in SUMO (U3) and \textbf{distributed coordination between traffic signals} (U4), in which each agent exchanges messages with its neighbours to build green waves and to prevent queues from blocking the neighbouring intersection (\textit{spillback}). It is evaluated on five synthetic cases of increasing difficulty: a two-intersection corridor under nominal (P1) and double demand (P2), a three-intersection network with a roundabout under nominal demand (P3) and 1.2 times the nominal demand (P4), and a nine-intersection grid (P5). The real intersection in Lima, imported from OpenStreetMap but not yet cleaned, remains as future work.

In every case, a fixed-time program tuned by grid search and SUMO's native actuated control are compared with tabular Q-learning and SARSA, three variants of IPPO, an independent PPO per signal (without messages, with neighbour messages and with a spillback penalty), Double DQN and MAPPO, with a centralised critic. All learners use the delay reward, are trained for 200 episodes with three base seeds and are evaluated with the policy that has the best validation score on thirty common seeds. The primary metric is the \textbf{delay per vehicle} (SUMO's \texttt{timeLoss}), and a predefined verdict rule classifies each model in each case according to its change against fixed-time and actuated control and its statistical significance.

The targets are to reduce delay by \textbf{25--40\%} and queues by at least \textbf{20\%} with respect to fixed time. IPPO with messages is the only model with a firm verdict in all five cases that fails in none: against fixed time it cuts delay by 6.8, 23.3, 8.8 and 3.6\% in P1 to P4 and ties with it in the grid (P5), and against actuated control it cuts delay by 3.6 to 16.4\% in all five cases. Without messages, IPPO ties or fails except under double demand; MAPPO matches IPPO with messages and Double DQN falls behind. Q-learning and SARSA fail in every nominal-demand case, and an ablation with a queue reward shows that the reward is not the cause. The delay target is not reached in any case; with IPPO with messages, the queue target is met only in P3 and P5. The work supports the claim that, in corridors and networks of two or three signals, a learned coordination with neighbour messages beats both classical references by a measurable margin, and that in a nine-intersection grid it matches a coordinated fixed-time program; not that it reaches the size of the targets.
